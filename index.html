Solar Economics in Denver

Denver's combination of high elevation, dry air, and 300 days of sunshine produces some of the highest solar irradiance values in the country — higher than Miami or Los Angeles. At current panel prices, rooftop solar in Denver generates electricity at roughly half the cost of buying it from the grid, with zero fuel cost and no price volatility. Here's what the data shows.

March 2026 · Data from NREL, EIA, Lazard, and EnergySage

The Physics

Why Denver Is Unusually Good for Solar

Solar panel output depends primarily on Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI) — the intensity of direct beam sunlight hitting a surface. DNI is reduced by atmospheric scattering: water vapor, aerosols, and the sheer depth of air the light passes through.

Denver sits at 5,280 feet. That puts it above roughly 15% of the atmosphere by mass. Less air means less Rayleigh scattering and fewer absorptive interactions. The result is measurable: Denver's annual average DNI is 6.12 kWh/m²/day, which exceeds Los Angeles (5.72) and Miami (5.04) by significant margins. Only the desert Southwest does better.

The second factor is humidity. Denver's semi-arid climate (annual average relative humidity ~40%) means far less water vapor in the optical path compared to subtropical cities. Miami's humid air scatters and absorbs a meaningful fraction of incoming solar radiation that Denver's dry air transmits.

Third, temperature. Solar panels lose roughly 0.3–0.5% of their rated output per degree Celsius above 25°C. Phoenix hits 45°C regularly; panels there operate 8–10% below nameplate on hot afternoons. Denver's cooler temperatures — even on sunny days — mean panels run closer to their rated efficiency. A panel in Denver producing the same irradiance as one in Phoenix will generate more electricity.

Cost Comparison

Solar vs. Natural Gas: What Electricity Actually Costs

Colorado's grid is powered primarily by natural gas (~40% of generation), with coal (~18%), wind (~25%), solar (~8%), and hydro (~4%) making up the rest. The average retail electricity rate in Denver is $0.16/kWh, or $160/MWh.

What does rooftop solar cost in comparison? A 10 kW system in Denver costs approximately $2.80/W installed, or $28,000. NREL's PVWatts model shows this system producing 1,652 kWh per kW per year. Over a 25-year lifespan with 0.5%/year degradation, that's roughly 38,800 kWh per kW — yielding a levelized cost of $72/MWh, with no subsidies.

That's less than half the retail grid rate. And unlike natural gas, solar has zero fuel cost and zero fuel price risk.

The cost trajectory matters

Solar module costs have fallen from $100/W in 1976 to under $0.10/W today — a thousandfold reduction driven by a 44% learning rate (cost drops 44% per doubling of cumulative production). In 2024 alone, the world deployed 446 GW of solar capacity — equivalent to the entire global nuclear fleet. Module prices have hit $0.07/W, below even aggressive forecasts.

Meanwhile, natural gas generation costs are dominated by fuel, which comprised $18.59/MWh of gas turbine operating expenses in 2024 (EIA). Gas prices are volatile: they spiked to over $9/MMBtu in 2022 before falling back to ~$2.50. Solar has no fuel input. Once installed, the marginal cost of the next kWh is zero.

At the retail level, Colorado electricity rates have risen roughly 3% annually. At that rate, today's $0.16/kWh becomes $0.29/kWh in 20 years. Solar locks your generation cost at zero for the life of the system.

The winter question

Denver averages 68% possible sunshine in December and January. For comparison: Seattle gets 25%, Chicago 38%, Boston 47%. A Denver winter day is sunnier than most cities' best season. December's DNI of 4.94 kWh/m²/day exceeds Chicago's annual average of 3.72. Snow on panels typically melts and slides off within 1–2 days due to the dark surface and panel tilt; annual production loss from snow is estimated at 2–5% on the Front Range.

Storage

Solar + Batteries: The Utilization Tradeoff

Solar alone achieves about 19% utilization in Denver — meaning a load connected directly to panels runs at full power roughly a fifth of the time. For some applications that's fine; for a home, you want electricity after dark.

Casey Handmer's analysis of solar+battery systems reveals a useful framework. Using real NREL 5-minute interval data and assuming $200k/MW for solar and $200k/MWh for batteries, he found two stable operating strategies:

Below ~$1,000/kW load capex: run intermittently on pure solar with modest array oversizing. No batteries needed. Utilization of 0.21–0.33. This is the regime for cheap industrial processes — Terraform Industries' synthetic fuel production lives here.

Above ~$1,000/kW load capex: batteries become cost-effective. Daily charge/discharge cycling pushes utilization to 0.9+. A typical home, with appliances ranging from $1,000–$2,000/kW equivalent capex, falls firmly in battery-backed territory.

What this means for a Denver home

A typical Denver household draws about 1.5 kW average (12,780 kWh/year ÷ 8,760 hours). A 13.5 kWh home battery (e.g., Tesla Powerwall) stores roughly 9 hours of average consumption, enough to bridge the gap between sunset and the next morning's solar ramp-up for most of the year.

Battery cell costs have hit $45/kWh — down from $700+/kWh in 2013 — driven by a 23% learning rate per doubling of deployment. Installed residential battery costs are higher (roughly $700–$900/kWh at the Powerwall level), but this margin is compressing as the market scales.

The key insight from Handmer's model: beyond about 15 kWh of battery per kW of load, additional storage gives diminishing returns. A battery sized for daily cycling gets excellent utilization. A battery sized to cover two consecutive cloudy days barely gets used. The more cost-effective path to higher reliability is solar array oversizing — it's cheaper to build more panels than to store electricity you might need once a year.

The 13-year payback is longer than the national average, primarily because Denver's retail rate ($0.16/kWh) is below the national average ($0.20/kWh). You're displacing cheaper electricity. But the economics still work: you're paying $0.072/kWh for something the grid charges $0.16 for, and the grid rate rises 3%/year while your solar cost is fixed at zero.

After payback, years 13–25 are pure savings — roughly $2,500+/year in avoided electricity costs, growing annually as grid rates increase. Many panels continue producing at 85%+ capacity through year 30–35.

Colorado incentives

Several state-level incentives improve the economics:

  • No sales tax on solar — saves ~$850 on a $29,400 system (2.9% state sales tax exemption)
  • Property tax exemption — solar adds ~$15,000 to home value, but Colorado doesn't tax the increase
  • Net metering — Xcel Energy credits excess production at full retail rate, rolling month to month
  • Xcel Battery Connect rebate — $350/kW upfront (up to $5,000) plus $100/year for 5 years for home batteries
  • RENU loan program — low-interest 20-year financing up to $75,000 for solar and energy upgrades

Note on the federal ITC: As of 2026, the federal solar Investment Tax Credit is no longer available for systems purchased with cash or loans. It may still apply through lease/PPA agreements — check with your installer.

Summary

The Data Supports It

Denver's physical geography — high elevation, dry air, 300 days of sunshine — delivers solar irradiance that exceeds most American cities, including ones with reputations for sun. The economics are straightforward: rooftop solar generates electricity at $0.072/kWh against a grid rate of $0.16/kWh that rises annually. Add batteries and the system becomes a near-complete substitute for grid power, with the cost trajectory firmly on solar's side.

The 13-year payback period is honest and real. It's not as aggressive as sunnier-but-more-expensive grid markets like California. But over 25 years, the net savings exceed $32,000, and the system keeps producing beyond that. Solar panels don't have moving parts. They don't need fuel. They just sit there and convert photons to electrons.
